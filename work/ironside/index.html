The brief

What a barbershop actually needs.

Most barbershop websites fail the same way: they're a template with a stock photo of a man in a cape, three paragraphs about "passion for the craft," and a phone number buried in the footer. They look like every other shop in the city.

But almost everyone landing on a barbershop site wants one of exactly three things: what does a cut cost, are you open right now, and how do I get an appointment. Everything else is decoration. So I built around those three, and spent the remaining effort on making it feel like a specific place rather than a generic one.

Decisions

Six choices, and why.

01

Dark and warm, not clean and corporate

Near-black background, cream text, amber and brick accents, a serif display face, and a fine film grain over everything.

Why: a barbershop sells a room you sit in for forty minutes. Bright white and blue reads like a dentist. The palette had to feel like leather, wood and warm light.
02

The price list reads like the one on the wall

Service name on the left, leader dots across, price on the right — the format of a printed menu, not a pricing table with feature checkmarks.

Why: people already know how to read this. It also signals "these are the prices, there is no package upsell," which is the actual promise.
03

Today's hours highlight themselves

The hours table checks the day of the week on load and marks the current row in amber.

Why: "are you open" is the single most common reason someone opens a local business site on a phone. Answering it without making them count rows is four lines of JavaScript.
04

The booking form is four fields

Name, phone, service, preferred day. That's it — no email, no address, no account, no "how did you hear about us."

Why: every extra field loses bookings. The shop only needs enough to text back and confirm; anything more is asking the customer to do the shop's admin.
05

Drawn, not photographed

The barber chair and the striped pole are hand-built SVG. No photo library, no image files at all.

Why: stock barbershop photos are instantly recognisable as stock, and they're the fastest way to look like a template. Vector art loads instantly, stays sharp on any screen, and belongs to this shop alone. Real client photos would replace it on a live job.
06

The phone number never leaves the screen

A brick-red bar pinned above the navigation carries the number and "walk-ins welcome" on every screen size.

Why: despite the booking form, most barbershop appointments still get made by phone. The form is for people who hate calling; the bar is for everyone else.
The numbers

Fast because there's nothing in it.

0External requests, fonts or CDNs
0Frameworks or libraries

Everything — layout, illustration, interaction — is one HTML file. No React, no jQuery, no font service, no image hosting, no cookie banner because there's nothing tracking anyone.

That's not minimalism for its own sake. A barbershop's customers are standing on a street corner on mobile data. A page that arrives in one request beats a beautiful page that arrives in forty.

Honest notes

What this is and isn't.

Ironside Barber Co. is not a real business. This is a concept build — a demonstration of how I'd approach a barbershop site, using invented details so I could design the whole thing rather than half of it. The reviews, the address and the barbers are placeholders.

On a real job, the structure and the craft stay exactly the same. What changes is that the photos, the prices, the names and the hours are yours, and the booking form posts to your inbox instead of showing a demo confirmation.
