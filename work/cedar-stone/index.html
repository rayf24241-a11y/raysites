The brief

Landscapers don't sell online. They sell estimates.

Nobody buys a patio off a website. What actually happens is: someone's yard has bothered them for two years, they finally search, they look at three companies, and they request a quote from one — maybe two.

So the entire job of the site is to be the one they pick to contact. That's a trust problem, not a beauty problem. Everything on the page is either building confidence or removing a reason to hesitate.

Which makes this the exact opposite design problem from the barbershop — and that's deliberate. Same builder, same 48 hours, completely different-looking result, because the businesses need different things.

Decisions

Six choices, and why.

01

Light, airy, and slightly boring

Off-white background, deep forest green, generous space, plain sans-serif type. No dark mode, no drama.

Why: the customer is about to let strangers with machinery onto their property and pay them thousands. They want to feel like they're hiring a real, established, insured company — not a cool one.
02

Prices are visible

Every service card carries a "From $45 / visit" or "From $3,200" figure.

Why: almost every contractor hides pricing to force a phone call. It backfires — people assume hidden means expensive and leave. A "from" number filters out the people who were never going to buy and reassures everyone else that they won't be ambushed.
03

The licence number is on the page

"Licensed, bonded & insured · OR LCB #9284" appears next to the quote form and again in the footer, with an offer to send the certificate.

Why: this is the specific anxiety. Everyone has heard a story about a contractor who took a deposit and vanished. Putting the number where the doubt lives is worth more than another photo.
04

The quote form gets the best real estate

It's a raised white card with its own section, its own heading, and three trust points sitting beside it. Five fields, and only three are required.

Why: it's the only conversion on the whole site. Everything above it exists to get people here, so it gets treated like the destination rather than tucked into a footer.
05

Illustrated projects instead of stock photos

Three drawn scenes — a retaining wall and resod, a paver patio with pergola, a planted front bed — each captioned with the real scope and how long it took.

Why: stock landscaping photos are obviously stock and quietly destroy the trust everything else is building. Illustrations are honest about being illustrations. On a real job these are replaced by the client's own before-and-afters, which are always better than anything you could buy.
06

The service area is written out

Six named neighbourhoods and suburbs in the footer, plus response times in the trust points.

Why: "do you even come to my area" is a silent bounce reason. Naming towns answers it instantly and gives the local search results something concrete to match against.

Honest notes

What this is and isn't.

Cedar & Stone is not a real company, and this is not client work. It is a fast practice build. The licence number, the reviews, the neighbourhoods and the testimonial are invented so I could design a complete page instead of a page full of "Lorem ipsum."

On a real job every one of those becomes the client's actual details — their licence, their photos, their towns, their reviews — and the quote form posts to their inbox instead of showing a demo confirmation. A paid job also gets considerably more time than this one did. The structure and the reasoning behind it stay identical — and a paid job gets considerably more time than this one did. Take this as the floor, not the ceiling.
